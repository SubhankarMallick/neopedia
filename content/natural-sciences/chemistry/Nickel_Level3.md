# Nickel : Level 3

## Abstract

Nickel (Atomic Number 28, Symbol: $\text{Ni}$) is a transition metal located in Group 10 and Period 4 of the periodic table. Extracted primarily from sulphide ores (like *Pentlandite*) and laterite ores (like *Garnierite*), it is known for its high strength, corrosion resistance, and ferromagnetic nature. Because of its unique catalytic properties and thermal stability, nickel plays a vital role in organic synthesis, stainless steel production, electroplating, and energy storage.

This article aims to delve into the properties, extraction and uses of nickel.

## Preparation

### Ores

Nickel is generally prepared from its sulphide and laterite ores.

- **Sulphide ores:** These kind of ores are generally found in Russia, Canada and China. The most important nickel ore is Pentlandite $[(\text{Ni}, \text{Fe})_9\text{S}_8]$, followed by Pyrrhotite ($\text{FeS}$ to $\text{Fe}_7\text{S}_8$) that contains a percentage of nickel as impurities.
- **Laterite ores:** Another class of nickel ores which are concentrated over millions of years by weathering of Peridotite which initially contains a small percentage of nickel. Over time, these nickel compounds may get concentrated enough in the ground to be profitable for commercial extraction. The major compounds involved are Garnierite $\mathrm{(Ni, Mg)_6Si_4O_{10}(OH)_8}$ which contains the most amount of nickel by mass but nickeliferous limonite $(Fe,Ni)O(OH)•nH_2O$ usually constitutes a greater portion of laterite ores.

### Extraction and Refining

Sulphide ores are generally crushed and then froth floatation process is conducted on them to concentrate the ore. Since nickel is ferromagnetic in nature, a number of its ores are also magnetic in nature, making magnetic separation viable in some cases. After the concentration process, the ore contains 6-12% nickel metal. The ore is subjected to leaching using sulphuric acid or ammonia. Nickel can form an artificial nickel-iron sulphide ore when heated to around $1350^\circ\text{C}$, called Matte, which has 25-45% nickel. After the creation of Matte, the iron is converted into an oxide, which combines with a silica based flux to form a slag. After the removal of the slag, the concentrate contains 70-75% nickel. Although direct conversion to metal is possible from this state, it is not performed due to the process needing a temperature of above $1600^\circ\text{C}$.

Usually, after this stage, the nickel metal is recovered from ammonia pressure leach, in which nickel is recovered from solution using hydrogen reduction, and the sulphur is recovered as ammonium sulfate for use as fertiliser. Electrorefining may also be conducted on pure nickel cathodes after conversion to chlorides or directly from sulphides.

The laterite based ores do not have sulphur and are usually oxides, making most concentration procedures ineffective. Usually, factories directly smelt the ore to pure metal at extremely high temperatures, while some factories also add sulphur into the ore to create Matte from which the procedure transposes into the sulphide based ore procedures.

## Uses of Nickel in Industries

Nickel is an important transition metal widely used in modern industries because of its high strength, resistance to corrosion, ability to withstand high temperatures, and useful catalytic properties. It is rarely used alone for structural purposes and is more commonly used in alloys, electroplating, batteries, and chemical industries.

One of the most important uses of nickel is in the manufacture of stainless steel. Nickel is added to iron and chromium to improve corrosion resistance, strength, toughness, and resistance to heat. Nickel-containing stainless steels are extensively used in kitchen equipment, medical instruments, chemical processing plants, food-processing equipment, pipelines, and construction materials. Nickel is also used in other important alloys such as Nichrome, which contains nickel and chromium and is used in heating elements because of its high electrical resistance and resistance to oxidation at high temperatures.

Nickel is widely used for electroplating. A thin layer of nickel is deposited on metals to provide protection against corrosion and wear and to give the surface a smooth and attractive finish. Nickel-plated components are used in automobile parts, machinery, household equipment, tools, and various engineering products. Nickel coatings can also serve as an underlayer for chromium plating.

Another major application is in the battery industry. Nickel is used in rechargeable batteries such as nickel-cadmium (Ni-Cd) and nickel-metal hydride (Ni-MH) batteries. Nickel-based battery technology has been used in portable electronic devices, power tools, emergency systems, and hybrid vehicles. Nickel-containing materials are also important in several modern rechargeable battery technologies.

Nickel has great importance in the chemical industry because of its catalytic properties. Finely divided nickel and Raney nickel are widely used as catalysts for the hydrogenation of organic compounds. For example, nickel catalyses the addition of hydrogen to carbon-carbon double bonds in vegetable oils. Nickel catalysts are also used in the reduction of nitro compounds, nitriles, aldehydes, ketones, and other organic substances.

Nickel is also important in the petroleum and petrochemical industries, where nickel-containing catalysts are used in several processes involving hydrogenation and conversion of hydrocarbons. Its ability to interact with hydrogen and organic molecules makes it valuable in industrial chemical processing.

Another important application of nickel is the Mond process, which is used to obtain highly pure nickel. Nickel reacts with carbon monoxide to form volatile nickel tetracarbonyl, $\text{Ni}(\text{CO})_4$, at a relatively low temperature. On heating, $\text{Ni}(\text{CO})_4$ decomposes to give pure nickel and carbon monoxide. This process is useful when very high-purity nickel is required.

Nickel-based superalloys are used in gas turbines, aircraft engines, power plants, and other high-temperature applications. These alloys retain their strength at elevated temperatures and resist oxidation and corrosion. Nickel alloys are therefore particularly important in aerospace and power-generation industries.

Nickel is also used in coins, magnets, welding materials, electrical components, and specialised engineering alloys. Its combination of mechanical strength, corrosion resistance, heat resistance, and chemical stability makes it an extremely valuable industrial metal.

Thus, nickel plays an important role in many areas of modern industry, ranging from stainless steel and electroplating to batteries, catalysis, aerospace engineering, and the production of high-purity metals.
