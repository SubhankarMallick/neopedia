# Article Style

So you have decided you want to contribute your knowledge and expertise to our cause. Thank you for your interest! Here are the details of how to write an article for the project.

## Rule 0: Every article is written in Markdown

This is a very important point. We use Markdown because it supports rich formatting as well as LaTeX for mathematical formulas. Additionally, it reduces security risks on our website, as Markdown itself cannot execute arbitrary code in the way that some other formats can. Moreover, it allows us to maintain uniformity across all articles.

## Rule 1: The grading system

Since our objective is to ensure that every person can understand a certain topic without requiring a PhD in that topic, we have a grading system for every topic. They are as follows:

- **Level 1** — Equivalent to middle school (Classes 6–8 or equivalent)

- **Level 2** — Equivalent to secondary school (Classes 9–10 or equivalent)

- **Level 3** — Equivalent to senior secondary school (Classes 11–12 or equivalent)

- **Level 4** — Equivalent to an undergraduate level (Preparing for or having completed a Bachelor's degree, or equivalent)

- **Level 5** — Equivalent to a graduate level (Preparing for or having completed a Master's degree, or equivalent)

- **Level 6** — Equivalent to a PhD level (Conducting or having conducted research, writing or having written research papers, or working at a comparable level)

Please indicate the level for which you are writing the article clearly. It isn't necessary for you to write the complete article for all levels. Every single level helps.

## Rule 2: Assumptions

As declared in Rule 1, the same level system applies to the assumptions made within an article.

You may assume familiarity with the concepts covered in previous levels. For instance, if you are writing a Level 3 article, you may assume that the reader is familiar with the information covered in Levels 1 and 2.

However, do not assume that the reader knows anything above the current level. Continuing with the same example, a Level 3 article should not assume knowledge from Level 4 or higher.

You should also avoid assuming that the reader already understands the current level's material before explaining it.

## Rule 3: No contradictions

Often, in educational situations, we learn about a theory or model only to learn later that it was incomplete, inaccurate, or replaced by a more accurate theory.

The rule is: **DO NOT unnecessarily contradict or invalidate information presented at a lower level.**

For instance, let us consider "Atomic Structure" in chemistry. A Level 1 article may introduce Rutherford's atomic model. However, the model is not completely accurate, and later models provide a more accurate description of atomic structure.

A Level 1 article should **NOT** tell the reader that the model they have just learned is "wrong" or immediately introduce a more advanced model that is unsuitable for their level.

This rule may be broken if the more accurate or advanced theory is appropriate for the current level and is explained within the same article.

When a higher-level explanation improves upon or replaces a simpler model, the transition should be explained rather than simply declaring the earlier explanation to be wrong.

## Rule 4: Format

The format you must follow is as follows:

- **Article name and level** — Use a Heading 1 in the following format:  
  `[Article name] : Level [level number (1 to 6)]`

- **Introduction / Abstract** — Use "Introduction" for Levels 1 and 2, and "Abstract" for Levels 3–6.

- **Main content** — You may arrange this as you wish. Long paragraphs? Fine. Tables? Fine. Images? Also fine! Choose the format that best explains the subject.

- **Summary / Conclusion** — Use "Summary" for Levels 1 and 2, and "Conclusion" for Levels 3–6. This section should wrap up the information presented in the article.

- **Bibliography / References** — Use "Bibliography" for Levels 1 and 2, and "References" for Levels 3–6.

- **Article Info** — This section should contain the names of the author(s), editor(s), proofreader(s), relevant dates, and any other applicable information about the article.

### Why use these specific words for different levels?

Grades 6–10 generally require simpler language and explanations. From Grade 11 onward, students increasingly enter a more academic environment where they are expected to write more formal papers, projects, and reports.

By using terms such as "Abstract," "Conclusion," and "References" at higher levels, Neopedia also aims to familiarize readers with the conventions of academic writing.

## Rule 5: No plagiarism or AI-generated writing

Since this is an open-source project under the GPL v3 license, we must ensure that we do not copy another person's work word for word.

If you really want to use a particular explanation or statement from another source, quote it appropriately and mention the source. Quotes should be kept short and should generally not exceed two sentences.

Whenever possible, explain ideas in your own words and cite the sources you used to verify the information.

### AI-generated writing

**AI may not be used to write the articles.**

Neopedia is intended to contain knowledge written and reviewed by real contributors. AI-generated articles therefore should not be submitted as original contributions.

However, AI may be used for other tasks related to an article, such as:

- Checking factual correctness

- Identifying possible errors or inconsistencies

- Suggesting areas that may require clarification

- Checking grammar or spelling

- Helping with Markdown or LaTeX formatting

- Assisting with technical aspects of preparing or publishing an article

The final article must be written and approved by human contributors.
